\begin{abstract}
The HAR-RV model of \citet{corsi2009} is widely reported as
difficult to beat in volatility forecasting; recent machine-learning
benchmarks have produced mixed results. We present a controlled
walk-forward comparison of five models---GARCH(1,1), HAR-RV, XGBoost,
LSTM, and a Transformer encoder---on NIFTY-50 daily realized volatility
from September 2007 through June 2026 (4{,}585 trading days; 3{,}500+
out-of-sample forecasts per model). Evaluating with the QLIKE loss
\citep{patton2011} and Diebold--Mariano tests of equal predictive
accuracy \citep{dieboldmariano1995}, we find: (i)~GARCH is significantly
worse than HAR-RV ($DM=-21.7$, $p<10^{-4}$), reproducing the standard
result in the Indian market; (ii)~XGBoost on engineered HAR-style
features is statistically indistinguishable from HAR-RV ($DM=-0.71$,
$p=0.48$); (iii)~LSTM and Transformer architectures \emph{both
significantly outperform} HAR-RV under QLIKE, with improvements of
roughly 4\% ($DM=+5.78$, $p<10^{-4}$ for LSTM; $DM=+2.23$, $p=0.026$
for the Transformer). The headline takeaway is that the often-cited
result ``machine learning fails to beat HAR-RV'' applies to tabular
ML on engineered features, not to sequence models that consume the
raw return and realized-variance series directly. Architecture
choice---not the classical-versus-modern dichotomy---is the
discriminating factor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Volatility forecasting is a cornerstone problem in quantitative finance.
Forecasts of conditional return variance are required as inputs to
option-pricing models \citep{black1973pricing}, value-at-risk and
expected-shortfall computations, and a wide range of allocation and
hedging procedures \citep{andersen2006volatility}. The literature spans
nearly four decades, anchored by \citet{engle1982arch} and
\citet{bollerslev1986garch}, and refined through asymmetric and
long-memory variants such as EGARCH \citep{nelson1991egarch} and
Realized GARCH \citep{hansen2012realized}.

Since the introduction of \emph{realized variance} estimators
\citep{andersen2003modeling}, the dominant benchmark in volatility
forecasting has been the Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of realized
variance (HAR-RV) of \citet{corsi2009}. HAR-RV is a parsimonious linear
regression on lagged daily, weekly, and monthly aggregates of realized
variance. Its persistent empirical advantage has motivated a substantial
literature attempting to beat it with richer specifications or
machine-learning models \citep{audrino2020machine, christensen2023machine}.

The picture from that literature is mixed: some studies report that
gradient-boosted trees or neural networks reduce forecast error;
others find machine learning fails to add value over HAR
\citep{christensen2023machine}. A recurring critique is that
comparisons are not always like-for-like: test windows differ, loss
functions vary, and the choice between tabular and sequence-based ML
architectures is rarely controlled.

The Indian equity market is underrepresented in this literature.
Volatility forecasting studies on NIFTY-50 have largely focused on
classical GARCH-family specifications; comprehensive deep-learning
benchmarks remain limited. Filling this gap is useful because Indian
markets exhibit distinct features---pronounced overnight effects,
sharp regime shifts around policy and election cycles---that make
them a non-trivial test bed for sequence models.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A controlled walk-forward benchmark of five models on
  18+~years of NIFTY-50 daily data, with a uniform initial training
  window, evaluation loss, and significance test framework across
  all models, and a refit cadence chosen to balance staleness against
  compute cost per model class.
  \item Diebold--Mariano significance tests of pairwise predictive
  accuracy against HAR-RV for each competitor.
  \item A reproducible open-source implementation that includes data
  ingestion, all five models, the evaluation harness, and analysis
  notebooks.\footnote{Source code:
  \url{https://github.com/manavmishra-cloud/nifty-vol-forecast}}
  \item The empirical finding that LSTM and Transformer
  architectures---but not XGBoost on engineered HAR-style
  features---significantly beat HAR-RV. The result clarifies that
  the often-cited ``ML cannot beat HAR'' claim is sensitive to the
  choice of architecture, not to the choice of model family
  (classical versus learned).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Classical conditional-variance models.} The ARCH family
\citep{engle1982arch, bollerslev1986garch} models the conditional
variance of returns as a function of past squared returns and lagged
variance. Asymmetric variants such as EGARCH \citep{nelson1991egarch}
and GJR-GARCH \citep{glosten1993relation} capture the leverage effect.
These models remain the practical default in many risk-management
contexts.

\paragraph{Realized-variance models.} \citet{andersen2003modeling}
introduce realized variance as the sum of intraday squared returns, an
observable proxy for latent variance. When only daily OHLC data is
available, range-based estimators serve as low-frequency substitutes:
Parkinson \citep{parkinson1980}, Garman-Klass \citep{garman1980}, Rogers
and Satchell \citep{rogers1991}, and Yang and Zhang \citep{yang2000}.
The HAR-RV specification of \citet{corsi2009} regresses log realized
variance on its lagged daily, weekly, and monthly averages, and
\citet{hansen2012realized} couples returns and realized measures.

\paragraph{Machine learning in volatility forecasting.}
\citet{christensen2023machine} provide an extensive out-of-sample
comparison of tree-based and neural network methods against
HAR-family specifications, and find that machine learning rarely
improves on a well-specified HAR-RV on US equity data. Several
earlier studies report improvements from boosted trees or recurrent
networks on individual stock or index volatility, but results are
heterogeneous across markets, sample windows, and loss functions.
The mixed picture motivates the present like-for-like comparison.

\paragraph{Deep learning in financial time series.} Beyond volatility,
sequence models have been applied to return prediction
\citep{sirignano2019universal}, option pricing
\citep{horvath2021deep}, and limit order book modeling
\citep{briola2020deep}. \citet{vaswani2017attention} introduce the
Transformer architecture used here.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use daily OHLCV for the NIFTY-50 index symbol \texttt{\textasciicircum NSEI}
from Yahoo Finance, covering 2007-09-18 through 2026-06-01
(4{,}585 trading days, 4{,}584 with computable Yang-Zhang realized
variance). NIFTY-50 is the broad-based Indian large-cap equity index
maintained by the National Stock Exchange of India.

\paragraph{Realized variance estimator.} As we have only daily OHLCV,
we use the range-based Yang-Zhang estimator \citep{yang2000}:
\begin{align}
\sigma^2_{YZ,t} &= \sigma^2_{O,t} + k \sigma^2_{C,t} + (1-k) \sigma^2_{RS,t}
\end{align}
where $\sigma^2_{O,t} = (\log(O_t/C_{t-1}))^2$ is the overnight
component, $\sigma^2_{C,t} = (\log(C_t/O_t))^2$ is the open-to-close
component, $\sigma^2_{RS,t}$ is the Rogers-Satchell estimator
\citep{rogers1991}, and $k = 0.34$ as recommended in the original
paper. Yang-Zhang is drift-robust and combines overnight and intraday
information, making it the preferred low-frequency proxy.

We forecast log realized variance one trading day ahead, i.e.,
$y_t = \log \sigma^2_{YZ,t+1}$. The log transformation stabilizes
variance scale and ensures predictions are positive after
exponentiation. Stylized features of the series---volatility
clustering, the leverage effect, and fat-tailed returns---are
present and consistent with the global literature.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{GARCH(1,1).} Implemented via the \texttt{arch}
Python package \citep{sheppard2023arch}. Returns are scaled to percent
for numerical stability; conditional variance forecasts are scaled
back to fractional units after one-step-ahead prediction.

\paragraph{HAR-RV (baseline).} Following \citet{corsi2009}, we fit
\begin{align}
\log \mathrm{RV}_{t+1} = c + \beta_d \log \mathrm{RV}^{(d)}_t
                          + \beta_w \log \mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t
                          + \beta_m \log \mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t
                          + \varepsilon_{t+1},
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t = \tfrac{1}{5} \sum_{i=0}^{4} \mathrm{RV}_{t-i}$
and $\mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t = \tfrac{1}{22} \sum_{i=0}^{21} \mathrm{RV}_{t-i}$.
Estimated by OLS at each refit.

\paragraph{XGBoost.} An \texttt{XGBRegressor} with 300 trees, max depth
4, learning rate 0.05, subsampling and column subsampling of 0.8. The
feature panel comprises lagged log $\mathrm{RV}$ at lags $\{1,2,3,5,10,22\}$,
HAR aggregates, lagged returns, range-based estimator lags, rolling
return statistics over 5 and 22 days, and day-of-week / month-of-year
dummies---a total of 16 features.

\paragraph{LSTM.} A two-layer LSTM with hidden size 64 and dropout
0.2, followed by a two-layer MLP head with ReLU activations. Inputs
are 22-day windows of four daily features: log return, log
Yang-Zhang RV, log Parkinson RV, log Garman-Klass RV. Trained with
Adam ($\eta=10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$) and MSE loss on a
standardized target; the standardization is undone at inference.

\paragraph{Transformer.} An encoder-only Transformer with
sinusoidal positional encoding \citep{vaswani2017attention},
two encoder layers, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=64$, four attention heads,
feed-forward width 128, dropout 0.2, GELU activations. Input
sequences and training schedule match those of the LSTM. We apply
LayerNorm before the prediction head.

\subsection{Walk-forward evaluation}

For each model, an initial training window of 1{,}000 trading days
(approximately four years) is used; predictions are produced
one-step-ahead with an expanding training window. The classical and
tree-based models (HAR-RV, GARCH, XGBoost) are refit every 22 days
($\approx$ one month) to balance staleness against compute cost; the
deep models are refit every 250 days ($\approx$ one trading year) as
neural-network retraining is substantially more expensive. The HAR-RV
walk-forward variant is refit at every step (linear-regression
estimation is essentially free).

Walk-forward CV is strict: a forecast produced at step $t$ is
estimated using only data with index $<\!t$. Targets are forward-
looking ($y_t = \log \mathrm{RV}_{t+1}$), so the deep models hold out the
last 15\% of their training window as a temporal validation split
for early stopping (patience 10 epochs).

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

\paragraph{QLIKE loss.} Following \citet{patton2011}, we evaluate
forecasts using QLIKE:
\begin{align}
L^{\mathrm{QLIKE}}(\hat\sigma^2_t, \sigma^2_t)
 = \frac{\sigma^2_t}{\hat\sigma^2_t} - \log \frac{\sigma^2_t}{\hat\sigma^2_t} - 1.
\end{align}
QLIKE is robust to noisy proxies of latent variance, penalizes
underprediction more than overprediction, and is the standard loss
in the modern volatility forecasting literature.

\paragraph{Diebold-Mariano test.} For pairwise comparison we use
the Diebold-Mariano test \citep{dieboldmariano1995} on squared log
forecast errors. Under the null of equal predictive accuracy, the
DM statistic is asymptotically standard normal. We use Newey-West
HAC variance with $h=1$ lag, matching the one-step-ahead horizon.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main forecast comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes one-day-ahead forecast quality
across the five models. HAR-RV achieves QLIKE 0.625, narrowly
outperforming GARCH(1,1) (0.646) and beating XGBoost (0.710) by a
wider margin. Both deep sequence models improve on the HAR baseline:
LSTM achieves QLIKE 0.600, and Transformer 0.599.

Three observations.

\paragraph{GARCH is significantly worse than HAR-RV.} The DM
statistic of $-21.68$ is overwhelming evidence that, in the Indian
market as elsewhere, the realized-variance approach dominates the
classical conditional-variance specification on daily data.

\paragraph{Tabular ML on engineered features does not beat HAR-RV.}
XGBoost, trained on the same feature panel that informs HAR plus
range estimators and calendar effects, produces forecasts
statistically indistinguishable from HAR ($p=0.48$). This reproduces
the result emphasized by \citet{christensen2023machine}: well-
specified HAR is hard to beat with off-the-shelf tabular ML.

\paragraph{Deep sequence models do beat HAR-RV.} Both LSTM and
Transformer architectures consuming the raw return and RV series
directly significantly outperform HAR. The LSTM result is
particularly robust ($DM=+5.78$, $p<10^{-4}$); the Transformer is
significant at the 5\% level. QLIKE improvements are around 4\%,
which is modest in magnitude but highly significant given 3{,}500+
out-of-sample forecasts.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The headline takeaway is that the often-cited result ``ML cannot beat
HAR-RV'' is a statement about \emph{architecture}, not about classical
versus modern. When we feed engineered HAR-style features into a
boosted-tree regressor, performance is statistically identical to HAR.
When we feed the raw daily return and realized-variance series into a
recurrent or attention-based sequence model, performance improves
significantly.

A natural interpretation is that HAR-style hand-engineered features
already capture most of the information available in lagged
realized variance, and that boosted trees cannot extract more.
Sequence models, by contrast, can learn nonlinear and longer-range
dependencies---asymmetric reactions to large returns, regime-
dependent persistence, and interactions between range estimators
that the linear HAR specification cannot represent.

\paragraph{Robustness considerations.} The result holds under our
specific choice of evaluation window, refit cadence, hyperparameters,
and input features. We have not yet conducted ablation studies on
sequence length or model capacity, nor evaluated on alternative
horizons (5-day, 22-day) or alternative realized variance estimators.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The realized-variance proxy is itself noisy
when computed from daily OHLC data. Access to intraday data would
allow direct realized variance computation \citep{andersen2003modeling}
and likely reduce the QLIKE floor across all models. The
Yang-Zhang estimator we use is the strongest available
low-frequency substitute.

The economic significance of a 4\% improvement in QLIKE warrants
careful interpretation. The QLIKE metric measures forecast quality
in a loss that combines proportional and log-ratio terms; a 4\%
reduction at one-day horizon translates to a modest but non-trivial
sharpening of variance forecasts. In risk-management applications,
particularly tail-loss estimates conditioned on forecast variance,
the gain is the most meaningful; in option pricing, the implied-
volatility-level effect is smaller and harder to monetize without
careful transaction-cost analysis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have benchmarked five volatility forecasting models---GARCH,
HAR-RV, XGBoost, LSTM, and Transformer---on NIFTY-50 daily data
over 18 years with strict walk-forward cross-validation. Both deep
sequence models significantly beat HAR-RV under the QLIKE loss
($p<10^{-4}$ for LSTM, $p=0.026$ for Transformer), while tabular ML
on engineered features does not ($p=0.48$). The well-known
difficulty of beating HAR-RV is a statement about feature engineering
and model architecture, not about ML in general; sequence models
that consume raw lag series extract useful signal that engineered
HAR features and tabular learners do not capture.

\paragraph{Future work.} Natural extensions are: (i) multi-horizon
forecasting (5 and 22 days); (ii) regime-conditional performance
analysis around crisis windows; (iii) inclusion of macro covariates
(India VIX, FII/DII flows, oil and INR/USD); (iv) intraday data and
direct realized-variance computation; and (v) interpretability
analysis (which input features the LSTM and Transformer weight
most).

scripts, and analysis notebooks are available at

NIFTY-50 OHLCV data are obtained from Yahoo Finance.
